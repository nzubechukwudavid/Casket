[Heading1] ABSTRACT
Phishing has become a major cybersecurity threat, which has greatly intensified by the use of AI to mock realistic fraudulent emails. Traditional detection methods that are based on static rules and keyword filters have become very ineffective against these adaptive attacks. This study proposes an AI-powered phishing detection system which integrates context-aware Natural Language Processing (NLP), adaptive machine learning, and behavioral analytics to improve accuracy and resilience. A dataset of 81,486 emails in total from six different sources including real-world, AI-generated, and traditional phishing samples was used for the sake of this study. The system uses a pre-trained BERT model for semantic and contextual understanding, combined with an adaptive learning pipeline to address the changes in phishing techniques over the years. Combination with classifiers such as (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, LSTM, BiLSTM) achieved accuracy between 85–98%, while the pre-trained BERT improved performance to 99.27% accuracy and achieved an F1-score of 0.9930. The best results were obtained through Ensemble Learning, where BERT + LightGBM obtained an accuracy of 99.82%, BERT + SVC achieved 99.42% accuracy, 0.995 precision, 0.995 recall, and an F1-score of 0.994 showing a higher balance and generalization. To confirm the reliability of these results, independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were carried out. The t-test showed that the ensemble models which involved classifiers significantly outperformed standalone models (p < 0.01), while ANOVA showed meaningful differences across all model types (p < 0.001). Post-hoc Tukey’s HSD confirmed that BERT-based ensembles consistently achieved significant improvements in  the precision and recall.Overall, the proposed BERT + SVC ensemble offers a statistically validated, scalable, and context-sensitive defense mechanism that perform better than some  existing models such as RNN-LSTM (98.8%) and Deep Q-Networks (F1 = 0.950), establishing a robust approach for solving evolving email  phishing problems.
Keywords: Phishing Detection, BERT, LightGBM, Machine Learning, Cybersecurity, Ensemble Learning, NLP, Statistical Validation, ANOVA, T-Test, AI Security

[Heading2] 1.1 Background to the Study
Phishing remains the most pervasive cyber menace, expanding in scale and complexity. It began life as crude email scams and has since developed into complex social engineering attacks based on psychological, contextual, and increasingly technical weaknesses (Proofpoint, 2024). Phishing has been described as the action of tricking individuals into disclosing sensitive data such as passwords, bank account numbers, or identification data, under the guise of official communication. As organisations become increasingly reliant on digital infrastructure, the implications of phishing have grown beyond individual data theft to large-scale breaches, financial losses, and reputational damage (Abiodun, 2021). This further confirms the inventiveness of attackers; it also reflects weak points in reactive frameworks of cybersecurity.
Traditional methods for detecting phishing, such as blacklists, heuristic rules, and basic keywordbased filters, are insufficient in coping with modern phishing threats. These conventional systems primarily rely on static databases of known malicious links or rigid syntactic patterns, which are easily bypassed by adversaries employing dynamic and intelligent content generation (Mafaz, 2021). The folly of static defences is perhaps best illustrated with artificial intelligence-powered phishing attacks. Today's attackers are already leveraging behemoth language models such as GPT-4 to craft emails not only naturally sounding but also contextualised with hints, making detection even harder.
Unlike the generic spam of the past, AI-phishing emails exhibit a refined understanding of grammar, tone, and even organisational hierarchies. This allows them to successfully impersonate institutions like banks, universities, or departments within companies (Chidinma et al. 2025). Furthermore, attackers proceed to personalise these emails further by looking into an online history of a victim (from social media usage or prior emails) so that they can craft messages that actually sound genuine (Nadeem et al. 2025). Consequently, such messages cannot be easily distinguished from genuine messages even by sophisticated users. Sophistication of phishing is sufficient to describe the big watermark in the evolution of these attacks, which were once static, but are now in adaptive, dynamic threats. This has created a big gap between attacker sophistication and the range of present defence mechanisms.
Recent statistics spotlight the ever increasing prevalence and complexity of phishing attacks. For example, the spread of phishing attempts across some industries (Figure 1.1) shows that media production recorded close to 2.91 phishing attacks per reported user, which is closely followed by government and manufacturing sectors. This furthermore shows that attackers intentionally target industries with very useful data, sensitive data and high user engagement.

While AI is increasingly being used in cybercrime, the dominance of human-authored phishing underscores the continued reliance on psychological manipulation and social engineering (Hong, 2012).

However, organizational assets, corporate emails, networks, and intellectual property remain major targets due to their potential for financial and reputational damage (Bada, Sasse, & Nurse, 2019).

The solution for such an effort is growing among technologists and researchers who are turning towards the promise of artificial intelligence to fill detection gaps. Conversely, it needs to be updated to combat probable attacks. Current research has attempted to bypass this problem by employing advanced machine learning and deep learning methods to the issue of phishing detection. ChatSpamDetector was suggested, utilising GPT-4 to detect suspicious patterns in email bodies with comparatively high accuracy (Koide et al. 2024). However, the model was limited in scope as it primarily focused on content analysis, leaving out other important elements such as sender metadata, user interaction history, and behavioural trends. Similarly, a transformer-based phishing system for detecting phishing was proposed, utilising the DistilBERT model coupled with LIME for interpretability (Uddin and Sarker 2024). Even though this method improved interpretability, it lacked proof of real-world deployment, and the efficiency in adapting to evolving attacking patterns was dubious. The border was enlarged by proposing an information theory framework which employed deep learning to derive phishing-significant features from email content (Nguyen et al. 2024).
Although this enhances the clarity of classification results, the performance of the model on diverse sets of data and evolving attack scenarios continues to remain untested in its entirety. A related concern is how existing phishing detection algorithms would be inadequate to catch AI-generated phishing emails since they are of superior linguistic quality and contextually indistinguishable from normal emails (Eze and Shamir 2024). They call for adaptive learning systems, which should be continually updated to keep up with emerging threats. Continuous learning models, therefore, become the main foci of innovation, specifically in environments where static detection becomes quickly untrustworthy.

Even the best models are plagued by high false positives when tested on actual datasets, which in turn decreases user trust and hinders the system's deployment. Moreover, the majority of phishing detection models also fail to consider "concept drift", i.e., continuous change in the data distribution due to continually evolving phishing methods, leading to a decrease in performance over time.
Other contributions from the literature include the use of behaviour data, including response behaviour, reading time, and patterns of user clicks, in enhancing models for phishing detection. With this body of work, it becomes evident that the future of phishing detection systems should transcend the old, content-based methods. An effective solution will have to bring together content analysis, adaptive machine learning, user behaviour modelling, and context awareness in a bid to keep pace with an ever-changing threat landscape. To facilitate this, this research presents an effective, AI-powered phishing detection system that marries contextual NLP, behavioural analysis, and adaptive learning to improve phishing detection. This system will not only be able to identify patterns of phishing fraudulent writing, but also deal with new forms of phishing attack techniques dynamically, thus providing an effective defence mechanism against advanced cyber-attacks.
[Heading2] 1.2 Problem Statement
The ever-growing complexity of AI-based phishing attacks through artificially created messages and advanced social engineering methods creates a major threat to conventional cybersecurity systems. Conventional detection methods based primarily on static features, rule-based ideas, and signature detection are not likely to develop contextual intelligence and flexibility in detecting sophisticated phishing e-mails mimicking genuine communication (Alkhalil et al., 2021; Eze & Shamir, 2024). Furthermore, the majority of the systems that have been implemented are low in false positives and degrade in performance over time since they are not capable of dealing with concept drift and evolving phishing tactics (Burita et al., 2021; Naqvi et al., 2023).
Phishing messages generated using AI can evade detection mechanisms by using misleading writing styles and exploiting users' behavioural traits. Despite recent advances in deep learning and detection models based on NLP, the majority of studies still fail to provide completeness in handling the entire spectrum of phishing attacks, particularly those involving contextual manipulation and behavioural deception. Therefore, what is essentially needed is a more context-sensitive, dynamic, and smart phishing detection model that can reduce false positives, improve accuracy, and be effective against dynamically evolving phishing tactics.

The proposed system tries to improve detection accuracy and robustness by incorporating behavioural analysis, dynamic learning mechanisms, and context-aware natural language processing (NLP).

To integrate adaptive machine learning techniques that address concept drift and enable the detection system to evolve alongside phishing trends.
To evaluate the performance of the proposed detection system using key performance indicators such as accuracy, precision, recall, and false-positive rate.
To compare the proposed phishing detection system against existing techniques to assess its effectiveness in real-world application scenarios.
[Heading2] 1.5 Justification for the Study
Cyberattacks, specifically phishing, are constant. Both in the breadth and the sophistication levels, the threat is an increasing one. With more and more aspects of daily life and business being conducted online, threats concerning phishing attacks have become exponentially more urgent. Generative AI has now made it possible for hackers to create realistic and context-based phishing emails in large volumes. Such a scenario makes many of the detection methods being employed currently obsolete or ineffective.
Phishing detection has also been attempted recently using new models like ChatSpamDetector by Koide et al. (2024) and transformer-based models by Uddin & Sarker (2024), showcasing the capability of AI in this regard. Still, the majority of the models are text analysis-specific or lack practical, real-world scalability and resilience. They also seldom utilise behavioural data, which is extremely crucial in observing the way users respond towards fraudulent emails.
This work, therefore, addresses a strong research gap in building a hybrid solution by combining context-aware NLP, adaptive ML, and behaviour analysis. The generated solution is expected to reduce false positives considerably and improve real-time detection capability, thereby becoming more feasible to apply in real-world, dynamic environments.
[Heading2] 1.6 Scope of the Study
The focus of this study is on the development and evaluation of an AI-powered phishing detection system. The core areas covered include:
Data collection and preprocessing of various  phishing and legitimate emails.
The Implementation of NLP models that understand context and language usage patterns.
The Integration of adaptive Machine learning models that continuously learn from new data.
Behavioral analytics based on user email interaction patterns.
Experimental testing using benchmark datasets and performance comparison with previously existing methods.
The research does not cover phishing detection in multimedia formats (e.g., voice phishing or smishing), nor does it aim to develop universal solutions for all forms of cyberattacks. [Heading2] 1.7 Methodology
The research design is experimental in nature, focusing on improving phishing email detection using diverse datasets and advanced machine learning techniques.
Some publicly available datasets such as CEAS 2008, Enron, Ling-Spam, Nazario, SpamAssassin, and Nigerian 419 were used to make sure there is variety in content, writing styles, and phishing characteristics noticed.
These datasets were cleaned and standardized, combining the key fields (subject and body), the texts were converted to  lowercase, and all duplicates and noise were removed
Feature extraction was done using TF-IDF for traditional models and BERT embeddings used for contextual deep learning models.
Multiple algorithms (classifiers) were trained, which included Logistic Regression, SVM, Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, LSTM, BiLSTM, and pre-trained BERT.
Ensemble models combining BERT with the aforementioned traditional classifiers were also implemented to improve  prediction.
An adaptive learning mechanism  was done using the Population Stability Index (PSI) which was applied to monitor data drift and cause model retraining when significant changes occurred.
The Model performance was evaluated using  metrics such as Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, AUC, MCC, Kappa, Specificity, FPR, and FNR
T-test and Anova statistical inferential analysis was also carried out to ensure the reliability of the results
Comparative analysis which was done across datasets and models identified BERT-based and ensemble approaches as the most effective for phishing detection in dynamic environment

[Heading2] LITERATURE REVIEW
Phishing attacks have evolved, with the most advanced technology being used to trick users and bypass traditional security mechanisms. The Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies, particularly Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL), have been central in constructing smart phishing detectors. The current literature review considers various approaches, methodologies, strengths, and limitations.
The field of overlap between email security and artificial intelligence is fertile ground for emerging strategies, each having some degree of success or limitation.
[Heading2] 2.1The Conceptual Framework of AI-Driven Phishing Detection
This section establishes the foundational concepts underpinning the proposed Phishing Email Detection System, defining the technical hierarchy from broad Artificial Intelligence to specialized Adaptive Machine Learning, and contextualizing each domain within the unique demands of modern cybersecurity. The integration of these advanced concepts forms the Theoretical Framework upon which the system’s novel efficacy is built.
Phishing
Phishing is a very known form of deceptive cyber attack which entails malicious persona impersonating trusted companies such as a bank, a cloud service provider or the IT department of a company, the main goal of phishing is to deceive victims to give out sensitive data inclusing login credentials or major financial details of the company, the effectiveness of phishing highly depends on social engineering and exploiting human psychological behavior such as trust, urgency or fear to bear critical judgement (Hong, 2012). Before now, it was easy to detect phishing through simple keywords and obvious malicious links  but the attackers have fast evolved  into  sophisticated Spear Phishing and convincing campaigns, identified by deep personalization and camouflage techniques (Jagatic et al., 2007).The consistent unpredictable nature of phishing  called Concept Drift  makes static, rule-based defenses inefficient, demanding a dynamic defense mechanism capable of determining the contextual intent behind the language used, rather than just scanning for signatures.
Artificial Intelligence (AI)
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a scientific field that is majorly dedicated to simulating or copying human reasoning processes such as learning, decision making, perception , reasoning  in computer systems (Russell & Norvig, 2021). In cybersecurity A1 depicts a major shift from finding and avoiding common threats to looking out for and classifying new threats. AI’s role is to make sure built detection systems are able to handle wide and complex data streams, by making available the major intelligence that is needed to classify the input, calculate risk probabilities and execute complex workflows without the need for explicit manual programming for every new form of threat, this ensures a smart defense system is built against phishing.
Machine Learning (ML)
Machine learning is a pertinent subside of AI that gives a practical method and framework to achieve intelligent behaviour, It allows systems to learn patterns and predictive functions from big datasets using statistical and computational methods which in turn improve their performance on specific tasks by using iterative experience (Bishop, 2006).Although classical Machine learning  algorithms, such as  Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forest (RF), have been widely used as comparative benchmarks in phishing detection, their success is very much  dependent on solid Feature Engineering which is the process of manually defining and extracting relevant indicators of phishing  from the raw email text. The major limitation of conventional ML is its heavy dependence on human expertise to define the feature space, a vulnerability that attackers exploit by developing novel forms of deceptive strategies that fall outside the defined boundaries.
Deep Learning (DL)
Deep Learning (DL) is an advanced evolution of Machine Learning, known by its use of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) containing multiple hidden layers hence the "deep" designation [Goodfellow et al., 2016). The primary solution offered by Deep Learning is its capacity for automatic feature abstraction. Unlike traditional ML, a DL model, such as a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) or Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), can by itself identify, extract, and model highly complex, hierarchical features directly from raw data (e.g., character sequences or raw word tokens) without the need for human intervention. This ability is important for analyzing the complex, unstructured text found in phishing emails, allowing the system to identify subtle semantic relationships and structural anomalies that are invisible to other ML models.
Natural Language Processing (NLP)
Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the technology that closes the gap between human language and computational understanding, by allowing computers to understand, interpret, and generate human language (Manning & Schütze, 1999). In the subject of phishing detection, NLP is the major mechanism responsible for processing the textual input (email body, subject line, links). It involves transformations such as tokenization or breaking texts into smaller units, lemmatization and syntactic parsing and these transformation processes convert unstructured human language texts into numerical data that Artificial intelligent models can work with. The transition from simple keyword based NLP to advanced contextual NLP is what has made it easier to detect these sophisticated social engineering attacks.
AI-Powered Contextual Analysis
AI-Powered Contextual Analysis represents the core innovation in advanced phishing defense, overcoming the limitations of traditional NLP and becoming the primary mechanism for detecting highly deceptive content. This approach is powered by Transformer architectures such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) (Devlin et al., 2019). The major function is to interpret the intent and the meaning of each word based on its context within a sentence instead of pointing it out in isolation without full contextual knowledge. It is then easy to understand the full meaning of a message and it can flash and email not just because of special words that indicate phishing. Instead it looks at every other part of the email message before indicating a phishing attack that then directly tackles social engineering at the linguistic level. This contextual understanding is the key to achieving the superior accuracy demonstrated by the proposed system.
Adaptive Machine Learning
Adaptive Machine Learning is a dynamic, operational necessity for any security system operating in a highly evolving environment, defining the methodology used to solve Concept Drift (Gama et al., 2014). Concept Drift occurs when properties of the target threat said to  define the  characteristics of phishing changes over time, causing static models to become outdated. Adaptive Machine learning solves this problem by using a continuous retraining and it ensures this by using a continuous loop. The system implements some performance monitoring which uses some statistical m methods such as Population Stability Index (PSI) to detect when real world data distribution is majorly different from the training data (Lu et al., 2018). When a drift is detected, the system automatically calls for new Data Collection through techniques like GPT-4 Augmentation) and starts to retrain the model. This ensures that the detection system’s parameters are constantly updated to remain robust and effective against the latest, most sophisticated, and emerging phishing methodologies.
[Heading2] 2.2Traditional Machine Learning Approaches
Early phishing detection systems primarily relied on traditional ML algorithms. For instance, Alhogail and Alsabih (2021) utilised Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) combined with supervised learning to detect phishing emails. While effective in controlled environments, their model faced challenges with computational scalability and lacked real-world validation (Kyaw et al. 2023).
Similarly, Al-Hamar et al. (2021) suggest a supervised learning-based spear-phishing detection model with feature selection. Although the model showed promise, it was not adaptive in real time and was limited by the fact that it was tested on enterprise-level datasets and not under adversarial testing.

Bountakas and Xenakis (2023) proposed a hybrid ensemble learning approach to identify phishing emails. While it was highly accurate, the model suffered from a high false positive rate and poor interpretability, which complicates debugging procedures.
While traditional approaches opened up the scope for automated detection, their application to phishing has become inadequate due to dynamicity.
[Heading2] 2.3Deep Learning and Transformer-Based Models
The advent of DL has revolutionised phishing detection. Koide et al. (2024) introduced ChatSpamDetector, a GPT-4-based AI detection model that demonstrated high accuracy. However, the model required extensive computational resources and lacked exploration of real-time deployment challenges.
Uddin and Sarker (2024) proposed an explainable phishing detection model using a transformer-based DistilBERT model combined with Explainable-AI techniques like LIME. While the model improved interpretability, it faced issues with processing overhead and scalability in diverse email environments.
The models advocate that both content analysis and explainability are important, although their realistic application is still a challenge.
Nguyen et al. (2024) introduced an information theory-based approach for phishing detection using deep learning. Their model required further validation across varied datasets and had limited real-world application data used in validation.
[Heading2] 2.4Ensemble and Hybrid Models
Ensemble learning techniques have been employed to enhance phishing detection accuracy. Fajar et al. (2024) conducted a comparative study of CatBoost, XGBoost, and Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM) models. They emphasised the significance of feature selection and model interpretability, finding that XGBoost was efficient for large datasets, while CatBoost maintained high accuracy with fewer features (Fajar et al., 2024).
Aslam et al. (2024) developed AntiPhishStack, an LSTM-based stacked generalisation model for optimised phishing URL detection. Their two-phase model combined base ML classifiers with a two-layered stacked LSTM network, achieving notable accuracy. However, the model's complexity could hinder practical deployment. (Aslam et al., 2024).
Ensemble learning remains one of the most promising frontiers, especially since it can improve performance through model diversity.

They performed a systematic literature review of Business Email Compromise phishing detection using ML. They emphasised the need for models that can learn new phishing methods and highlighted the importance of diversity in datasets. (Atlam and Oluwatimilehin, 2023).
[Heading2] 2.6Real-Time Detection and Practical Deployment
Real-time phishing detection remains a significant challenge. A defence strategy against spam and phishing emails was developed using an evolutionary game theory model. While theoretically robust, the model's complexity limited its practical deployment (Wang and Song, 2021).
Also, an ML-based phishing detection system was developed using K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), and Random Forest (RF). Despite achieving high accuracy, the model's integration into real-time applications remained untested, and it did not account for adversarial attacks (Onih 2024).
A study published in the Journal of Information Security and Applications (2023) presented a high-accuracy phishing website detection method based on ML. Utilising algorithms like Logistic Regression, KNN, Naive Bayes, RF, SVM, and XGBoost, the model achieved over 93% accuracy. However, real-time deployment and adversarial robustness were not addressed. (Bahaghigat et al. 2023).
[Heading2] 2.7Adversarial Resilience and Explainability
Phishing detection systems are under threat from adversarial machine learning. Adversaries can potentially construct inputs that will be able to mislead models, and therefore, very robust defences are required. Tests have demonstrated that perturbations cause misclassifications and write off the necessity for adversarially robust models (Nadeem et al. 2025).
Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques have been used in phishing prediction models to provide more transparency. Uddin and Sarker (2024), for instance, applied LIME and Transformer Interpretation to explain the model's prediction for the purpose of allowing understanding as well as trust. (Uddin and Sarker, 2024).
Even with such advancements, much remains to be known regarding training, testing, and deploying phishing-detection models.
[Heading2] 2.8Challenges and Future Directions
Despite advancements, several challenges persist:
Scalability: Many models struggle to maintain performance with large-scale data.
Real-Time Detection: Implementing models that can detect phishing attempts in real-time remains complex.
Adversarial Robustness: Models need to be resilient against adversarial attacks that can manipulate inputs.
Explainability: Enhancing model transparency is crucial for user trust and regulatory compliance.
Dataset Diversity: Models require training on diverse datasets to generalise effectively across different phishing strategies.
2.9Summary of Reviewed Literature
The literature review shows a continuous evolution and improvement in phishing detection research, starting at static feature based algorithms, to intelligent adaptive and context aware machine learning models. Earlier studies relied heavily on the regular classifiers such as Decision Trees, and Random Forests, Naïve Bayes, Support Vector Machines (SVM), these depended heavily on hand written feature extraction and rule based logic to differentiate phishing emails from human emails (Singh et al., 2021; Ahmed et al., 2021).
Even though these methods have achieved a notable accuracy and they exhibited some limited adaptability to new patterns of phishing and poor performances in real time or changing environment.The emergence of deep learning models especially Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) and BiLSTM architectures marked a major advancement in the field allowing systems to learn sequential and semantic dependencies directly from textual and metadata inputs (Senouci & Benaouda, 2025; Aslam et al., 2024).
Other breakthroughs were gotten by the introduction of some transformer-based models such as GPT, BERT, RoBERTa, which leveraged attention mechanisms to detect subtle cues in email content and metadata (Altwaijry et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). These architectures have improved the ability to interpret and robustness, which allows the phishing detectors to identify the deceptive linguistic patterns, inconsistent context and hidden signals that static models usually miss. Very much recently reinforcement learning and large language model systems have become adaptive phishing countermeasures. Studies by Nguyen, Childress, and Yin (2025) and Xue et al. (2025) show examples of this shift,
Introducing debate-driven and adversarially trained multi-agent frameworks that enable autonomous reasoning, negotiation, and some self-correction when phishing classification happens, Jabbar and Al-Janabi (2025) shows the effectiveness of RL-based training in improving model adaptability using reward-driven optimization.
Other  research has also used  hybrid and ensemble techniques that combine deep learning with explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) tools such as SHAP and LIME to balance  the interpretability  with the predictive power (Uddin & Sarker, 2024; Daniel et al., 2025). Also, feature selection and data augmentation methods such as Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), Principal Component Analysis (PCA), and SMOTE have also been used to reduce dimensionality and address dataset imbalance (Farooq et al., 2022; Fajar et al., 2024). In spite of these achievements, major gaps still exist in current approaches. A lot of  studies are lacking  real-world validation on large-scale and exhibit limited multiple lingual capability, and do not  incorporate contextual signals from sender-receiver relationships, behavioral metadata, or domain-specific cues (Naqvi et al., 2023; Jaiswal et al., 2022).
These constant limitations  show the need for AI-powered contextual analysis and adaptive machine learning frameworks that have the ability to evolve in response to growing phishing tactics. These systems should combine linguistic behavioral and environmental context to be able to understand not just content but as well the intentions behind the communicators. Thai research builds on existing works by proposing a context aware and dynamic learning AI driven approach to phishing detection, which enhances scalability, interpretability and resilience in the face of increasing phishing attacks

[Heading2] 2.10Technological Change with Phishing Techniques
Nowadays, the extensive potential of the digital age makes enormous differences in cyberspace for threats. It just evolved from simple scamming phishing emails into much more sophisticated attacks with multiple layers. Furthermore, the introduction of NLG and AI-enabled bots has made phishing attackers capable of impersonating distinctly human-type conversations while generating 'identical' phishing messages perfectly grammatically relevant and contextually right. With AI at the front end, advanced persistent threats (APT) create large nationwide email phishing campaigns that keep adapting to user behaviour over time (Sharma & Gupta, 2024). Therefore, advanced detection systems would be required to understand not only the contents but also the context and intent.
[Heading2] 2.11Impact of Human Factors in Phishing Vulnerability
While the technical defences are critical, there are also human factors that contribute to phishing vulnerability. Emails containing urgency, fear, authority, and curiosity are the most common psychological triggers by which phishers catch their victims. Training and awareness campaigns have been suggested; however, studies have shown that even educated individuals can fall victim to the sophisticated applications of social engineering (Singh et al., 2021). Behavioural analytics can be integrated into the detection systems for catching anomalies based on user habits, which tend to be reliable indicators of deception.
[Heading2] 2.12Integration with Email Systems for Real-Time Implementation
The other main consideration is the actualisation of the phishing-proof systems into the existing enterprise email infrastructures, like Microsoft Outlook or Gmail, which could even be enterprise-specific services. The deployment challenges faced include real-time scanning, low-latency response, user alerts, and feedback mechanisms. AI-powered plug-ins with cloud-based models are scalable solutions but need extensive evaluation with adversarial samples and system compatibility issues (Zahra & Hafeez, 2024).
[Heading2] 2.13Limitations of Contemporary Research Paradigms
Despite remarkable advancements, existing phishing detection models suffer from a variety of limitations. For example, one major challenge is the absence of longitudinal testing, where models are deployed and subjected to evaluation over long periods of time to observe deterioration of performance with time. Another gap is the lack of diversity in training datasets, for many studies depend on pre-labelled, outdated datasets, which do not reflect the dynamic nature of modern phishing attacks. Only a few include feedback loops or user corrections by which to adapt models to evolving tactics (Iqbal & Abbas, 2022).
[Heading2] 2.14Context-Aware AI Models
New research shows that context-aware systems possess even greater potential than not only to leverage textual data but also to take into account user history, device metadata, time of day, geolocation, and previous communication to detect anomalies in emails.
By establishing an email profile with a more comprehensive representation of emails, detection systems may be able to distinguish it with much greater accuracy when it is genuine or not. This, however, requires highly complex user permissions and data engineering mechanisms to ensure compliance with privacy (Farooq et al., 2022).

[Heading3] 3.2.1System Overview
The module-based phishing email detection system established in this research paper is modelled in the form of a pipeline, which combines computational linguistics, situational analysis and personalised learning. The flow of the system is systematic, such that data ingestion leads to the classification with periodic updates of the model.
The input source is a compilation of email data that has been scraped or gathered by other means, such as publicly known phishing bodies and by means of large-scale email. The emails are then processed in a pipeline comprising cleaning, anonymisation, tokenisation, and formatting the input to fit into the BERT architecture. When the emails are ready to be fed into the BERT architecture, they are passed to a fine-tuned BERT model. In the model, the token representation of any email is through [CLS], where we learn contextualised semantics and use a classification head to predict the label in the email. The result is a probability distribution of the phishing and non-phishing classes, subsequently permitting the system to achieve precise binary inference.
The system has an adaptive learning component so as to keep the system effective in the long run. The module automatically monitors the current data, monitors the model performance, and re-trains on new collected data when a performance drop or change in pattern is detected. Such flexibility is essential to ensure resiliency against adopting phishing methods.

[Heading3] 3.3.2Data Cleaning and Preprocessing
To clean up the raw email texts to make good use of them in training and analysing the model, a synthesised crawling pipeline was used as follows:
Lowercasing: The text in all the emails was also translated to lowercase to reduce completeness and eliminate the issue of case sensitivity during tokenisation and training of the model.
Replacement of URLs: All found URLs (that is, URLs in email body) are detected by regex and exchanged on a token url_rand, which is a standardised. This will keep the visibility of hyperlinks without tainting information such as dynamic or sensitive content.
Email Address Replacement: Email addresses were also anonymised by way of replacing them with the token _email_ oo to anonymise personal information, but retain the contextual relevance.
Special Character Removal: Non-alphanumeric characters that do not contribute meaningful semantic information (e.g., symbols and punctuation marks) were removed, except for characters like quotes and underscores that may carry syntactic importance.
Preservation of Placeholder Tokens: A custom filtering mechanism was applied to ensure that placeholder tokens such as _url_ and _email_ remained intact, while unrelated underscores were stripped from other parts of the text.
Whitespace Normalisation: Sequential whitespace characters were collapsed to a single space, and leading/end spaces were dropped to have a clean and consistent document structure.
This preprocessing method provided the dataset with a uniform standard, no noise and an ideal representation to address to NLP model, without compromising valuable indicators towards phishing behaviour.

[Heading3] 3.4.1Input Formatting
Prior to feeding text data into BERT to classify phishing emails, the data must be preprocessed and put in the right format so that it can feed input to the model.
Figure 3.4: Input Formatting Process for BERT-Based Phishing Email Classification
This intermediate step involves the tokenisation of words utilising the WordPiece tokeniser that divides the words into subwords to address the out-of-vocabulary terms, as illustrated in Figure 3.4. The use of special tokens is then introduced: a [CLS] token is added to the start of each input sequence to signal it as part of a classification, and a [SEP] token is also added to the end of the sequence to signal its termination, or to delimit the beginning of the pair when used, in which case. Each token is then mapped into its equivalent input ID, dependent on the BERT vocabulary. An interest mask is also produced to distinguish between the real tokens (marked as having 1) and padding tokens (marked as having 0) so that only meaningful input can be seen by the model during training. Also, token type IDs are established to differ between sentence parts in sentence-pair tasks, whereas in single-sequence tasks, such as phishing detection, the token type IDs are all 0. Lastly, defragmentation is done such that input sequences are sliced or truncated into a fixed maximum length to process them efficiently in batches. All this formatting is done to ensure that, at the very least, the input data is in a manner which the BERT could interpret and learn.
[Heading3] 3.4.2Architecture Details: Transformer Layers and Classification Head
Transformer layers
Based on a Transformer encoder-based architecture, the BERT model also allows it to focus on contextual associations between terms in a paragraph, irrespective of their placement. The internal structure of a Transformer encoder block that is repeated Nx times in the architecture of BERT is depicted in Figure 3.5 above. Every block will include these fundamental blocks:
Figure 3.5: Architecture of Transformer Layers in the BERT Model
Input Embedding and Positional Encoding: Raw input tokens are then transformed to dense representations by using an embedding layer. The Transformer does not bring any knowledge of word order to the table; hence, positional encodings are added to the embeddings to incorporate sequential information of tokens.
Multi-Head Attention: This procedure implies that the model would be able to focus on different parts of the input simultaneously. It finds the scores of attention to all words in the sequence and makes the model comprehend relations between words--at both short and long distances, because it compounds the information of the multiple attention heads to be compounded.
Add & Norm: A residual (skip) connection is also placed around the output of the multi-head attention, followed by layer normalisation. This solidifies the training process and assists in retaining the original input signal and including learned attention features.
Feed-Forward Network: A fully connected feed-forward neural network has been used in each position independently. A common implementation is a cascade of two linear transformations separated by a ReLU activation, which allows these models to learn non-linear combinations.
Add & Norm (again): Like the previous normalisation step, a second residual connection and normalisation are followed by the feed-forward layer to further stabilise learning.
These, in conjunction, compose a single Transformer encoder layer. In BERT-Base, these have 12 such layers built on one another, which allows deep and bidirectional contextual understanding. Such architecture enables BERT to learn the word semantics of emails in the phishing classification task, particularly well, when fine-tuned on annotated data.

Classification Head
The pre-trained bert-base-unicased model of the Hugging Face transformers library was used in fine-tuning the task of phishing email classification. To make the phishing email detection system long-term resilient and flexible, this paper includes an adaptive machine learning pipeline. The idea is to make the model be able to continually learn emerging data patterns, particularly by changing the strategies used in phishing by the cyber attackers. Last but not least, the model is retrained using the modified dataset in order to rediscover its performance and then deployed again. [Heading3] 3.5.1Addressing Concept Drift with the Population Stability Index (PSI)
To create long-term stability in the functioning of the phishing detection model in a dynamic threat environment, the study incorporates the Population Stability Index (PSI) as a means of detecting a drift. PSI can be seen as a statistical metric to judge the variation in a key feature distribution between original training sets and incoming data sets gathered at deployment. This technique can assist in determining whether phishing trends have changed considerably to the extent that it necessitates retraining of models.
In this paper, PSI is applied to some chosen input attributes, such as vectors of token frequencies according to TF-IDF representations, and patterns of semantics identified in the email body. All the numeric features are usually discretised into a given number of bins, and categorical features are typically converted to a prespecified number of levels. PSI < 0.1: No significant shift (stable distribution)

In the context of this project, PSI is calculated periodically on sliding windows of recent emails. When the PSI value exceeds the 0.25 threshold for critical features (e.g., presence of phishing-related keywords, structure of sender domains), the system triggers the adaptive learning pipeline to collect new data and retrain the model. This approach allows for proactive adaptation of the model to novel phishing strategies, maintaining its reliability over time without manual intervention.
[Heading3] 3.5.2Model Retraining Pipeline: Periodic Updates using New Email Samples
With the signature-based model, a retraining pipeline needed to be set up so that the model would remain effective in an environment of phishing tactical changes. This pipeline allows periodic updates by allowing the addition of newly gathered email samples in the training process. Since the system constantly receives data, especially that which is generated in the real world, such emails are processed and added to the current data. At some point, after a new set of samples has been met or when a dramatic decay in performance is noted (e.g., via drift metrics), the model becomes retrained on a batch of old and new data. Such an approach will make the model adaptive, concept-drift resistant, and have the potential to learn and accurately recognise the new patterns of phishing with time.

Gradient Boosting (GB)
Gradient Boosting is an ensemble learning algorithm which creates models one by one, with each new model aiming to correct the errors made by the previous models. LightGBM (LGBM)
LightGBM is a superfast gradient boosting model engineered by Microsoft that is meant to be effective on large datasets. As opposed to traditional Gradient Boosting, where decision trees are built in a level-wise manner, LightGBM builds mmmm leaf-wise with depth limitations.
LightGBM comes in handy specifically when dealing with large email datasets (such as Enron, CEAS and Ling). It is effective in capturing and differentiating phishing patterns, even when attackers cause minor variations across the emails, due to its capability in capturing complex feature interactions. In addition, it is fast and scalable and can be deployed to real-world applications in cybersecurity systems where new phishing samples are being generated every day.
[Heading3] Deep Learning Models
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
LSTM is a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) variant that tries to learn long-range interactions in sequence data, which simple RNNs have difficulty doing because of vanishing gradients. LSTMs can identify temporal patterns within text (e.g., suspicious sequences of phrases, reused phishing signals in emails). They are especially handy in identifying phishing, with sequence context being essential.
Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM)
BiLSTM is an extension of LSTM and works with the sequence in both directions (forward and backwards), giving the model context about both past and future tokens.
Relevance of Detection in Phishing:
BiLSTM is capable of identifying some of the subtler phishing indicators using previous and following words in an email. To illustrate, to identify that your account has been compromised or that account creation was successful, you need to look at the surrounding tokens.

4.7Inferential Statistical Analysis: Application of T-Tests and ANOVAInferential statistics were used  in this research to check whether the performance differences observed among the algorithms, datasets, and ensemble designs were real or simply just the  result of random fluctuation. Since several models including BERT, BiLSTM, Logistic Regression, SVM, Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM were evaluated, inferential testing ensured that any apparent performance gaps were genuine and not just due to chance,which in turn  gives  stronger empirical credibility to the evaluation.
4.7.1Rationale for Inferential Testing
Descriptive statistics such as mean accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score usually help summarize model outcomes but these do not tell whether the differences among models are statistically meaningful. Inferential testing using t-tests and ANOVA gives that evidence by verifying if variations in model averages extend beyond random noise. These methods strengthen the study’s findings by adding formal statistical support to the observed trends.
4.7.2Independent Samples T-Test
The independent samples t-test was used to compare the average performance of two separate models or dataset. For instance, it evaluated whether the mean accuracy and F1-score of the BERT-only model was meaningfully different from those of the BERT + LightGBM ensemble. Each model was trained across several folds, and the resulting metrics were treated as samples used for analysis.
The hypotheses for the t-Test were formulated as follows
H₀ (null hypothesis): There was no meaningful difference in mean performance between the two presented models.
H₁: There was a meaningful difference between the two models.
The t-statistic was determined as:
t = (X̄₁ - X̄₂)  /  √((s₁² / n₁) + (s₂² / n₂))
Where X̄₁ and X̄₂ represent the sample means of model performance metrics, s₁² and s₂² arre the variances, and n₁ and n₂ are the number of evaluation runs for each model
A probability (p) value under 0.05 led to discarding the null hypothesis, confirming a statistically meaningful difference. Results showed that both BERT + LightGBM and BERT + SVC ensembles scored significantly higher on accuracy and F1-score than the standalone BERT and BiLSTM models (p < 0.01). These results demonstrate that the ensembles’ gains reflect true model improvement, not random variation.
4.7.3One-Way ANOVA
A one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was also used to determine whether three or more model types differed in their mean performance when put under identical conditions. It compared traditional ML (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost), deep learning (LSTM, BiLSTM, BERT), and ensemble (BERT + LightGBM, BERT + SVC) groups.
The hypotheses for the one-way ANOVA is as follows:
H₀ (Null Hypothesis): There is no statistically significant difference in the mean performance among the model groups.
H₁ (Alternative Hypothesis): In at least one model group there is a statistically significant difference in mean performance compared to the others.
The F-statistic for ANOVA was computed using the formula beow:
F = MS_between / MS_within
Where MS_between stands for the mean square between the model groups (variability due to model type differences), and MS_within represents the mean square within groups (variability due to random error).
A probability value less than 0.05 led to the discard of the null hypothesis, indicating that at least one group’s mean performance differed significantly from the others. In this study, the one-way ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences in model performance across all tested groups (p < 0.001), showing that model type had a substantial impact on phishing detection effectiveness.
Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test. To identify where the differences occurred.  The post-hoc analysis confirmed that the ensemble models (BERT + LightGBM and BERT + SVC) highly  outperformed both the traditional ML and standalone deep learning models across all measured  metrics, including accuracy, recall, and F1-score and  precision.
These findings furthermore show the superior performance of the ensemble architectures was not just by random variance but the improvement is attributable to the combination of the BERT text features with the classifiers.
4.7.4Interpretation and FindingsInferential analysis strengthened the credibility of the experimental results:
T-Test Outcomes: Ensemble models (BERT + LightGBM and BERT + SVC) obtained statistically higher mean scores compared to individual models (p < 0.01), showing genuine performance improvement.
ANOVA Outcomes: The ANOVA results (p < 0.001) confirmed noticeable performance variation among all model categories. Post-hoc analysis showed ensemble systems had the most consistent and superior performance across evaluation measures.4.7.5Implications for Phishing Detection ResearchThe inclusion of inferential statistical testing adds some scientific robustness to the study by demonstrating that the performance improvements of the proposed model can be replicated and are statistically valid. By rejecting the null hypotheses in most pairwise and group-wise tests, the results furthermore proves  that the gains in accuracy, recall, and F1-scores are truly attributable to the model architecture rather than dataset randomness.Moreover, employing inferential statistics aligns this study with the best practices in machine learning evaluation, where quantitative evidence is combined with formal hypothesis testing to ensure results can be reproduced  and are reliable. These findings prove the claim that AI-powered contextual analysis combined with adaptive ensemble learning is  a statistically superior method for phishing email detection.

[Heading2] 4.9Summary of Results
This chapter introduced training and evaluation of various phishing email detection models on eight benchmark datasets, emphasising the development of traditional machine learning classifiers, deep contextual embeddings, and ensembles. The early testing of benchmark models including Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), LightGBM (LGBM) and deep models like LSTM and BiLSTM have shown that although the models can perform reasonably well, their accuracies are usually limited to 8598 per cent, with some notable trade-offs in recall and precision between datasets. Afterwards, BERT fine-tuning on individual datasets improved the results of the classification significantly, with the highest accuracy observed at 99% on average, and large F1-scores and low error rates, which were substantiated by confusion matrices. The validation loss curve and the training loss curve showed that the convergence was stable with a lower chance of overfitting, which confirmed the strength of BERT in tackling complex phishing detection tasks.
Expanding on these findings, ensemble learning based on BERT embedding with the addition of classifiers, which included LR, SVC, RF, XGB and LGBM, further improved the performance of detection. In all datasets, the best ensemble variants performed better than standalone BERT, with BERT + LightGBM and BERT + SVC often being the best performers. These models not only show marginal increases in accuracy (up to 99.8) but also balanced precision, recall, and F1-score and kept false positives and false negatives down to the minimum.
Aggregating the results of all datasets ensembles proved to be more generalizable, with BERT + SVC (99.42) and BERT + LightGBM (99.39) being the best in terms of results. Analysis also established that ensemble techniques enhanced robustness among data imbalances that were reflected in smaller datasets, such as Ling and SpamAssassin, where base models failed.
Lastly, the evaluation of the BERT-based ensemble models against the existing literature showed that they exceeded the majority of previous studies, including modern deep learning models, like RNN-LSTM (Senouci, 2025) and Deep Q-Networks (Daniel et al., 2024). Although BERT-LSTM (Kalla et al., 2025) had a competitive accuracy (99.55%), the suggested BERT + SVC offered a better balance of all indicators, with the recall being the most essential in reducing false negatives in the case of phishing detection.

[Heading2] 5.1Summary
This study gave a solution to the current problem of phishing email detection in that an intelligent system based on natural language processing using contextual information, adaptive learning algorithms, and data augmentation was developed. The phishing attack has continued to be one of the most rampant and harmful types of cybercrimes, and attackers have continued to vary their tactics to ensure they get past the customary and rule-based systems, as well as static machine learning approaches to phishing detection. The motivation behind the proposed study was to develop a model that not only performs with a high success rate in the detection of phishing attacks, but it can be adjusted dynamically according to emerging new trends of phishing attacks.
The process commenced by collecting various datasets of phishing emails and legitimate emails, as well as phishing samples that were generated with the use of the Mistral language model. To mitigate the class imbalance issue and increase the ability of the models to generalise, the augmentation process was used to yield 1,000 synthetic phishing samples. A breakdown data in the dataset after augmentation is shown in a more detailed manner, and the total dataset reached the value of 82,486 examples.
In the data preprocessing phase, the duplicated data values in the rows and irrelevant stop words were eliminated, after which clean data was produced, consisting of 74,237 samples. This step was essential, since data quality is a key feature and may either uplift or throttle machine learning model performance. After cleaning, the data was split into training, validation and testing datasets.
The proposed model was trained in a supervised learning framework through the optimisation techniques, including gradient descent and learning rate scheduling techniques. Training outcomes showed convergence since the training loss became steadily reduced and started at the value of 0.6 and fell to 0.0006. Similarly, validation accuracy improved steadily with its training steps to a final accuracy better than 99%, indicating that the model was well-learned and overfitting had not reached a severe level.
Regarding the model evaluation, the system exhibited a high level of performance on the test set, recording 99.4 per cent accuracy, an F1 of 0.994 and a loss of 0.033. Its inference speed was also competent, with about 59 samples/sec being processed.
The comparison of the proposed system with other models proved to be better. The competing methods of Deep Q-Network, XGPO, RNN-LSTM and Stacked LSTM, although performed in detail, were not as precise, recalled and F1 as the proposed model. As an example, the F1 score was 0.950 with the Deep Q-Network, and 0.994 with the proposed model, which is a substantial gain. This makes the system a state-of-the-art solution to detecting contextual phishing.
The other major addition that this study brought was that of an adaptive learning mechanism. Traditional models can degrade over time as a result of concept drift, with the phishing tactics being developed outside the coverage of the training data. The adaptive methodology employed here means that the system can add new samples to the training loop it uses to be relevant and less susceptible to the advent of emerging threats.
All in all, the study met its aim of showing that contextual AI models coupled with augmentation and adaptive approaches bring great success in identifying phishing emails and are more accurate, efficient and adaptive than traditional approaches.
[Heading2] 5.2Recommendation
Based on the findings of this study, several recommendations are provided to guide practical implementation and further research:
Practical Deployment in Email Systems: The proposed phishing detection model should be deployed in organisational email systems and integrated into existing email gateways and spam filters. Doing so will provide an additional layer of defence against phishing attacks, reducing risks of data breaches and financial losses.
Continuous Model Updating: Since phishing techniques evolve rapidly, organisations should establish pipelines for continuous retraining of the model with fresh datasets. The adaptive learning module demonstrated in this work should be operationalised to ensure the model remains effective in real-world environments.
Combining Content and Metadata Features: While this study focused on the textual content of emails, future deployments should also analyse metadata such as sender IP, domain reputation, frequency of communication, and embedded URLs. This multimodal approach would significantly strengthen detection.
[Heading2] 5.3Conclusion
This project was able to design and test a phishing email detection system that performs better than other state-of-the-art systems. The robustness and reliability of the model are indicated by the observation that the precision, recall, and F1 score were values of 0.996, 0.995, and 0.994, respectively, by including contextual analysis, synthetic data augmentation, and adaptive learning. The results do more than confirm the effectiveness of the proposed system, as they also emphasise the relevance of continuous learning in the fight against cyber threats. This is unlike in the case of static models, which cannot adapt to new phishing techniques and thus are not sustainable, as they will serve no purpose in the long run. The comparative results confirmed that the model is better than the existing approaches, including Deep Q-Networks, RNN LSTM and XGPO. The research will thus contribute greatly even in the cybersecurity field since it offers a very adaptive, scalable, and accurate phishing detection system.
